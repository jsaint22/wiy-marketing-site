Here is a question I get more than almost any other, usually in a lowered voice, usually near the end of a first conversation: *How do I tell my CPA they've been missing this for years?*
It's a good question. It's also the wrong first question. The better one is: do you actually need to tell them anything at all, or do you need to change the shape of the conversation?
Let me tell you about a client who taught me the difference.

## Thirty years of trust, and a quarter million a year on the table
He had worked with the same CPA since college. Not figuratively — the CPA was literally his college roommate. Thirty years of returns, thirty years of trust, thirty years of "he's handled it and it's always been fine." The relationship was implicit and it was deep. Any time anyone raised a concern about the tax work, he got defensive fast. That loyalty was the dominant frame, and it was doing exactly what loyalty is supposed to do.
Then I reviewed his prior returns.
Two red flags surfaced almost immediately. The first: Real Estate Professional Status was a defensible, well-documented election given his activity level, and it wasn't being taken. The second: an S-corp election on one of his entities should have been in place years earlier, and wasn't.
Between the two, a substantial amount every year that should have been in his pocket was sitting on the table instead. Both items were missing from both years of returns I reviewed.
He didn't want to hear it.

## The move most advisors get wrong
Here is where the typical multi-disciplinary advisor makes a predictable mistake. They position against the existing professional. They frame it as incompetence. They make the client choose between the new advisor's analysis and thirty years of friendship. And when they force that choice, one of two things happens: the client defends the CPA and tunes out the advisor, or the client fires a friend and carries the guilt into every future meeting.
Neither of those is a good outcome. Both of them treat the CPA as an obstacle instead of a teammate.
So I did the opposite. I invited the CPA into our tax strategy meeting as a co-participant. Not to ambush him — to work the problem together, in the same room, with the illustrations on the table where all three of us could see them.
I walked through the REPS documentation and the S-corp illustration in front of both of them.

## What actually happened
Most advisors predict this moment badly. They assume the CPA will get defensive, dig in, protect his ego, protect the relationship by insisting he was right all along.
He didn't.
He was humble about it. He acknowledged the strategies were outside his typical scope. He didn't pretend otherwise, and he didn't get defensive. He said he'd research them. Then he went back, confirmed the analysis was sound, and offered to amend the prior returns himself — for free, as a friend.
Zoom out over the decades he has left to work and the difference compounds into something far larger than a single year's number. But that's not the part of this story I think about most.
The part I think about most is that the client kept the CPA. Thirty years of trust never got rupture-tested. The friendship survived. The CPA leveled up on a strategy area he hadn't worked in before. Everyone came out ahead.

## Why this is a model, not a one-off
There is a version of financial advice that runs on displacement. It grows by convincing you that everyone currently advising you is failing you, and that the fix is to consolidate everything under one roof — usually the roof that happens to charge a percentage of your assets to hold it.
I don't work that way, and this story is why.
The people already around you — your CPA, your attorney, your insurance agent — are not obstacles to be cleared. In most cases they're competent professionals doing competent work inside their scope. What's often missing isn't a replacement. It's coordination, and someone whose job is to see the whole board and elevate the specific places where elevation is needed.
When I take on a client, I'm not looking for reasons to fire the team. I'm looking for the gaps between the players on the team — because that's where the money usually is. The REPS election and the S-corp weren't missed because the CPA was bad at his job. They were missed because nobody's job was to look across the entire structure and ask whether the pieces fit together.

## So how do you actually tell your CPA?
Back to the question we started with.
You probably don't tell them anything, at least not the way you're imagining it. You don't walk in with an accusation. You change the shape of the room. You get everyone who touches your financial life at the same table, put the analysis where everyone can see it, and let the work speak.
A good professional, invited in as a collaborator rather than cornered as a defendant, will almost always rise to it. The ones who won't — the ones who get defensive when a defensible, documented strategy is put in front of them — have told you something important, and you'd want to know that too.
Ask your advisor this: *if you found something my current CPA had missed, would you invite them to the meeting, or would you use it to try to replace them?* The answer tells you what kind of relationship you're actually being offered.
